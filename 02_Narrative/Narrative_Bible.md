1. Core Premise and Themes
Premise: A legendary hero must confront the consequences of his choices. In PROJECT RUSTAM, the player assumes the role of Rostam, the mightiest Persian champion of legend, whose strength and victories are unmatched but whose life is defined by family, honor, and fate. The story explores Rostam’s journey from heroic adventures to the ultimate tragic confrontation with his own son, Sohrab.

Thematic Thesis: True heroism is tested not only by foes but by duty, love, and destiny. Rostam’s path highlights themes of fate vs. free will, the cost of pride, fatherhood and legacy, loyalty and betrayal, and sacrifice for one’s people and kin.

Tone & Genre: The tone is epic and gritty, blending high adventure with personal drama. Like the best action-adventures (e.g. God of War), combat should feel powerful and cinematic. But emotionally, the tone is sombre and reflective. The tragedy of Rostam–Sohrab must feel inevitable yet heart-wrenching. The world will be visually rich and mythic, with a mysterious undercurrent (similar in scope to Witcher 3 or Ghost of Tsushima, but uniquely Persian/Central Asian in aesthetic).

Player Fantasy: The player will experience Rostam as a superhuman champion yet relatable man. He must feel truly unmatched in battle, wielding mighty Persian weapons (bows, maces, axes, a magical mace like Rostam’s “Gurzin”), and riding the loyal warhorse Rakhsh. Between fights, the player will explore an ancient mythic Persia, solve environmental puzzles, undertake heroic quests, and shape Rostam’s relationships through dialogue and action. The fantasy is being a world-renowned hero whose strengths attract both glory and tragedy.

Narrative Pillars:

Heroic Action: Challenging combat encounters (atmospheric boss fights against demons, legendary beasts, and rival warriors) that highlight Rostam’s might.
Personal Drama: Emotional interactions and character moments (e.g. tender time with Tahmina or Zal, the shocking reveal of Sohrab’s identity) that root the epic in human stakes.
Epic Scope: Grand settings (sacred forests, fortress-demons’ lairs, mountain fortresses) and large-scale events (battles, sieges).
Cultural Immersion: A cohesive world drawn from Persian and Central Asian myth and history, with lore scattered in objects, architecture, and dialogue.
Hero’s Journey: Rostam grows (even as a legend) through challenges. The player should feel the weight of each major decision.
Non-Goals: We will not be simply re-playing the Shahnameh verse-by-verse. Nor will we degrade the culture to simple Orientalist tropes or mere spectacle. This is not a multiplayer or microtransaction game. We will avoid heavy emphasis on things like crafting or map clutter for the sake of it; narrative coherence and combat quality come first. We also will not propose an open-world MMORPG scale; scope must fit the eventual team size (likely a small to mid-sized dev team).

2. Setting & World-Building
2.1 Mythic Geography & Cosmology
PROJECT RUSTAM is set in a mythologized pre-Islamic Iran (Iranshahr). The Shahnameh mixes historical “pseudo-geography” with mythical places. We distinguish:

Mythic Iran: The core lands of Persia as in legend (Parsgan, Chorasan, Sistan/Zabulistan, Mazandaran, etc.). Cities like Ray, Khavaran, Kabul (ancient Kabul valley), and Samangan will appear. Political setting is the late mythical Kayanid era (roughly Bronze Age or early Iron Age, but timeline is legendary, not historical). The Kayanian kings (manuchehr, kay kavus, kay khosrow, etc.) rule from the royal city (Ray/Tabaristan).

Turan & Hamavaran: The lands of Persia’s main rivals. Turan (Central Asia / Uzbek steppe) is the realm of Afrasiab and the Turanian kings. Hamavaran often corresponds to Arabia/Egypt in medieval Persian imagination. These “other” realms will appear only in context (e.g. Hamavaran war with Afrasiab).

Mazandaran (Devil’s Land): A mysterious supernatural realm inhabited by Divs (demons). It is said (in the epic) to be unconquerable even by great ancient kings. Its exact location is “unclear”; scholars debate whether it was conceived as India, Levant, or Persian legends. We treat it as a legendary demon-kingdom reachable via magical routes. In game terms, Mazandaran’s fortress (Mazanderan-e Divan) will be the setting of the Seven Labours questline (Rostam’s trial path), with landscapes that shift from ghostly deserts to demon forests to underworld caves.

Religious/Cultural Context: The world is pagan-Zoroastrian (as in the epic) but full of supernatural elements. Zoroastrian gods are not explicitly patron characters, but the dualistic worldview (Ahura Mazda vs. Ahriman) underlies the legend: Rostam fights “evil” (Angra Mainyu/Divs) and honors heroes (Shahnameh describes Rostam praying to Ahura Mazda in a crisis). Mythological creatures (Simurgh, Divs, dragons) and folklore are literal truth here. Common people in villages may offer prayers, see omens, etc.

History vs. Legend: We follow the epic’s structure:

Mythical Age: The world was first created, monsters were slain (not our focus except maybe echoes, e.g. Simurgh escaping some calamity).
Heroic Age: The age of Rostam and Kayanian kings. This is our timeline. Any “historical” events (like wars against Alexander, Arabs) come in a later age and are irrelevant here.
Note: We avoid modern nationalistic overlays. Although Tajik/Persian heritage inspires us, we treat the setting as legendary Iran (Iranshahr) and Turan, not specifically modern Tajikistan.
2.2 Environment & Cultures
Terrain & Climate: Varied. Expect:

Mountains and Plateaus: High ranges (Alborz, Hindu Kush). We open the game with Rostam venturing out of Alborz foothills. (Our first major area could be mountain passes in Sistan or Zabul.) –– (Embed Image)
Snow-capped mountains and rugged terrain will define the backdrop of northern Zabulistan and the approach to Mazandaran. Photography of Tajik/Pamir peaks (above) suggests the kind of monumental scale. Rostam’s homeland is envisioned as highland plateaus and passes. This environment supports cinematic vistas and vertical exploration.

Deserts and Steppe: Central plains and desert (as in the thirst labour, a desert with a hidden spring).

Forests and Jungles: Mythic forests near Mazandaran (the Shahnameh mentions twisted forest creatures).

Oases and Rivers: The trek to Mazandaran includes a life-saving spring (the “ewe” leads Rostam to water). Visual design: rocky gulches with hidden pools.

Architectural Ruins and Strongholds: Ancient fortresses (some real-world inspiration: Sogdian ruins, Bactrian towers) and fanciful demon-fortresses (glowing runes, obsidian spires).

Cities: Rarely visited. Possibly a Persian capital (briefly Kay Kavus’s throne, maybe Iram of the Pillars if used). Kabul (Mehrab’s city) and Samangan are prominently mentioned in the story. We can model Samangan with tiered citadel and gardens (silk road style) for the Tahmina quest. Kabul may appear briefly during Zal’s era.

Society & Customs: Feudal-tribal. The Shahnameh depicts warrior nobility, kings, and a tribal ethos. Design notes:

Costumes/Armor: Classic Persian/Kayanian dress – patterned tunics, lamellar or scale armor, lamellar helmets with tall plumes. We should research Sassanid or Kushan patterns but give them a mythic flair.
Language: Characters will speak a stylized Persian-influenced English. Use Persian names (Rostam, Zal, Tahmina, etc.) consistently. Titles like “Shah” for king, “Mir”/“Khan” for tribal leader. Avoid modern idioms. Possibly sprinkle a few known Persian phrases (transliterated, e.g. “Khodâfahme” for farewell, though in dialogue we use English but with a formal register).
Behavior: Hospitality and honor are key. Rostam must be shown as magnanimous yet ferocious.
Crafts & Lore: Materials: bronze swords, Damascus-style steel, leathers, engraved metals. Architecture: brick and tile (ish), but more stone/demolished relics in wild areas.
Fauna: In addition to Rakhsh (the superhorse), include regional fauna like ibex, wolves, falcons (symbolic). Mythic animals (roc, karxindar [the horse-eating demon]) will appear in setpieces.
Magic/Myth: Real. The Simurgh (a giant benevolent bird) and Divs (demons) are part of the world. We portray Simurgh as luminous (glowing feathers), wise (elephantine beak). Demons vary from troll-like to serpentine. We must balance wonder and fear.
Contrast of Myth vs. History: Key here. We will mark any attempt to “locate” myth in history clearly: e.g., Mazandaran’s location is unknown in legend, so maps will show it as a sealed area/otherworld. Shahnameh Atlas notes it “was inhabited by godless magicians, conquerable only by divine power”; we echo that mystique. The story’s Iran is an Iranic cultural sphere, not the modern nation-state.

2.3 Audio/Music
Though not asked for explicitly, it’s part of world feel. The audio will draw on Persian/Central Asian motifs. Instruments like rubab, dutar, ney, daf may be used in lullaby or village scenes; more epic scenes use orchestra with subtle Persian scales. Voices: Characters speak English, but we should consider a heavy accent or use Persian terms. Battle cries might be in staccato, exclamations (e.g. “For Iran-zamin!” or “Ram!” (charge)). Sound design: horse gallop echoes, roars, echoing tombs.

3. Character Bible
Below are the main characters. (For each: Role=narrative function, Core Traits=personality, Arc=change during story, Relationships/Notes. All names are given in standardized transliteration.)

Rostam (Rustam) – Hero / Tragic Protagonist. Son of Zal and Rudabeh; marriage to Tahmina; father of Sohrab. Supreme warrior of Iran. Traits: Herculean strength, honor-bound, fiercely loyal to Iran’s king, devoted to family (though slow to express it). Prideful, stubborn, but capable of deep tenderness (e.g. with infant Siavash, with baby Sohrab once he realizes). Arc: Shifts from confident youth to world-weary hero. He wins almost every battle, but each victory costs something. His unknown paternity of Sohrab (until too late) haunts him. By the end he is shattered by grief, realizing that destiny (fate and manipulation) led him to slay his own son. Voice: Deep, commanding, formal. (Sample line: “I am Rostam, son of Zal; my axe is for enemies, not innocents.” (Original example)). Risks: Avoid making him a mustachioed cartoon tough-guy; emphasize his intelligence and kindness. Visuals: towering, muscular, long black beard and hair, often in stained armor from battle. Moves with thunderous weight.

Zal – Mentor / Father Figure. Rostam’s father. White-haired (albino) as a baby, raised by the Simurgh. Later a respected king. Traits: Wise, compassionate, gentle but strong in counsel. He knows many secrets (e.g. prophecy of Rostam & Tahmina’s child) and fears Rostam’s fate (warns him against Esfandiyar). Arc: From proud warrior to a worried old man who must watch sons make fatal choices. He tries to guide Rostam, but fate overwhelms. Voice: Soft, measured, poetic. Often a calming presence (until his voice nearly breaks when Sohrab is revealed). Risks: Not a bloodthirsty general – more the wise calm. Show tenderness with Rudabeh and admonitions to Rostam.

Rudabeh (Roodabeh) – Queen, Mother. Princess of Kabul, daughter of King Mehrab (descendant of Zahhak). Traits: Regal, passionate, rebellious (against her father’s forbiddance). She defied social norms for love. Arc: From anxious new bride to a proud mother. She endures the ordeal of bearing Rostam (nearly dying until Simurgh’s help). Voice: Lyrical, gentle but strong. In the narrative, she offers emotional counsel (“the compassion of a mother”). We may use her in flashback scenes (e.g. Rostam’s birth), and as narrative ‘polestar.’ Risks: Avoid fetishizing her as just “the beauty.” She is heroic in her own way (her labor story presages Rostam’s fate).

Rakhsh – Animal Companion. Rostam’s legendary horse. “Fiery-colored stallion” chosen by Rostam. Traits: Fierce, intelligent, almost sentient loyalty. Rakhsh has arguably saved Rostam’s life many times (as seen in the First Labour – killing the lion). Role: Player’s mount in travel and combat. Rakhsh’s survival is a personal stake for Rostam. Voice/Portrayal: Through animations (snorts, neighs) and reactions. Always with Rostam. Visually an enormous equally muscular horse with red-gold coat. Risks: Make him feel like a character (camera angle, Rakhsh whinnies when in danger, etc.) to deepen bond.

Tahmina (Tahmineh) – Love Interest / Mother of Sohrab. Daughter of Samangan’s king. She deliberately seeks out Rostam (falling in love with his legend). Traits: Assertive, wise, patriotic (wants a hero-child to save Iran). Arc: From bold suitor (she comes by night to Rostam) to loving wife left to raise Sohrab, to grieving mother. Voice: Confident, articulate (she recites poetry to seduce him in Shahnameh). Portrayal Risk: Avoid making her a passive damsel; she boldly initiates marriage. She should appear dignified with traditional noble attire (jewels, flowing robes, veil). The token of child (the jewel Rostam gave her) should be a key visual element.

Sohrab – Antagonist (Tragic Antagonist). Rostam’s son by Tahmina, though neither knows it at first. By adolescence, he is a warrior prince of Turan. Traits: Impetuous, noble, virtuous yet prideful. He inherits Rostam’s strength and Tahmina’s intelligence. He is loyal to Turan (since Afrasiab accepted him) but deeply yearns to meet Rostam. Arc: Starts eager to prove himself (rescuing slaves, fighting for Afrasiab), then moves to discover his father’s identity. His tragic flaw: acting on pride and youth — he fights Rostam without deeper inquiry (only learning truth as he dies). Voice: Young, ardent, dignified. He may recite part of his mother’s words to Rostam. Risks: Humanize him fully. He is not a “villain” but a pawn of fate and politics. The reveal of his heritage should be an emotional cutscene.

Kay Kavus – The King of Iran (Kayanian Shah). Ostentation-loving, arrogant. Traits: Easily swayed by flattery (the Div’s poem about Mazandaran), leading Iran into disastrous war. Arc: He blunders into being blinded by demons, then regrets and fears Rostam’s power. He oscillates between praising Rostam and fearing him (even tries to keep Rostam away from Sohrab). Voice: Petulant, dramatic. When things go wrong, he pouts and then blames others. He should be comical to a degree (like an incompetent ruler), but with moments of grief (learning of Sohrab). Portrayal Risk: Avoid making him cartoonishly evil; he is more foolish than villainous. Costuming: royal robes, crown, eyes often bandaged (after Mazandaran episode) or squinted.

Kay Khosrow – Youthful Allied Prince. Son of King Siyavash (martyred by Sudabeh) and Farangis, and grandson of Kay Kavus. Saved and raised by Rostam. Becomes Shah after Kavus. Role: Not central in Game I, but elder Rostam will protect/honor him. Possibly appears as boy or young man in epilogue/dialogue (Rostam swears fealty to Khosrow). Traits: Wise, compassionate (values Rostam’s counsel). Voice polite and kingly. Not fully needed in Bible but mentioned for completeness.

Afrasiyab – King of Turan. Iran’s great antagonist. Traits: Cunning, ruthless, ambitious to subdue Iran. He installs Sohrab (unbeknownst Rostam’s son) as agent against Kavus. Role: We may not see him directly (or only briefly); he is the unseen puppeteer in Sohrab’s story. Voice: Deep, commanding, maybe off-screen voice. He already killed many Iranian kings and sees Rostam as biggest threat. Portrayal Risk: He can remain a background threat. Possibly cameo at beginning or end.

Esfandiyar (Isfandiyar) – Kayanian Hero / Rival. Son of King Goshtasp, another warrior like Rostam (rival hero). Invincible to mortal weapons (blessed by Ahura Mazda). Role: In Shahnameh, Rostam and Esfandiyar’s fates clash (prophecy: one must die). This likely falls beyond Game I. But we mention his duel (Rostam kills him with Simurgh’s help) as future lore. Voice: Noble, somewhat bureaucratic (prays for justice). He obeys orders to eliminate Rostam. We might include a flashback or lead-in, but likely keep this for sequel planning.

Simurgh (Sīmurğ) – Mythical Phoenix-like Bird / Mentor. A giant benevolent bird. Raised Zal and visited Rostam. Traits: Vastly intelligent, prophetic, caring (calls Zal “son”). Provides guidance at crucial moments. Arc: Appears to Zal at Rostam’s birth (gives feather), later aids Rostam in battle with Esfandiyar (in that tale). Voice: Deep, otherworldly, female. Possibly communicated via vision or interpreting an omen. (We can depict her via a spectral form that speaks, or only through Zal’s memory/dream.) Risks: Not to overuse. She should appear in only key plot points (e.g. guiding Rostam at Tahmina’s chamber, or as memory when Rostam is desperate).

Other Figures (Brief):

Sudabeh: Kay Kavus’s wife, Sohrab’s stepmother, who lusts for Rostam and then falsely accuses him. She is the spark that sends Rostam to Turan war in the original tale. We may or may not include her. If we do, she will be a scheming queen (avoid making her too powerful; her role is secondary).
Shaghad: Rostam’s half-brother (Zal’s son by a maid). Ultimately betrays Rostam (not in first game). Not essential for Game I.
Olad (Allaudin): A master hunter whom Rostam rescues and later installs as viceroy of Mazandaran. Perhaps a strong NPC ally.
Gordafarid: A Turanian female warrior who briefly battles Sohrab (in the Shahnameh); not Rostam’s story. Minor.
Hojir: Son of Gudarz; captures Rostam’s identity and hides it from Sohrab. He is a cameo in the Sohrab battle (calls Rostam “Baluchistan’s lion”), but not needed in early development.
Divs/Creatures: White Div (Div-e Sepid, boss of Mazandaran); Arzhang (white div; Rostam’s slaughter); various labours’ monsters (lion, dragon, witch). Each should feel distinct: e.g. a giant sand serpent, a sorceress with demon minions, the White Div with armor of bone.
4. Narrative Architecture
We have not yet fixed the exact scope of Game I. The Shahnameh provides a full life story, but we must choose how much to cover. Major candidate structures:

A. Rostam’s Heroic Coming-of-Age (Labours as Climax):
Period: Young Rostam (18+ years) after taming Rakhsh, up through his greatest feat, the Seven Labours in Mazandaran. Ends when Rostam liberates King Kay Kavus (effectively concluding the Mazandaran saga). Sohrab not introduced (or only as a legend).
Pros: Clear quest (rescue the king), a defined final boss (White Div), satisfying heroic arc. Rostam’s personal life (Tahmina, Sohrab) can be foreshadowed or saved for later.
Cons: Leaves out the emotional father–son element that distinguishes Rostam’s story. Requires a sequel for the Sohrab tragedy (losing the biggest emotional hook).

B. Rostam through Sohrab’s Tragedy:
Period: Rostam from prime (post-labours) through his meeting with Tahmina and culminating in the fight with Sohrab (and Sohrab’s death). This is the “Rostam–Sohrab saga” arc. Could include flashbacks to Rostam’s labours as prologue, or have labours be part 1, Sohrab part 2.
Pros: Contains the central tragedy of his story in one game. High emotional stakes, making the game memorable. Rostam’s heroic feats frame the emotional core.
Cons: Very heavy tone (child death). Perhaps less action variety if we skip the Mazandaran demon-fights, or we could treat labours as optional side quests. Also, leaving Mazandaran (with White Div) out seems a waste of the set-up. This scope is quite long: many main conflicts (Mazandaran, war with Turan).

C. Anthology of Feats:
Period: Set-piece vignettes (like God of War II structure) focusing on separate episodes: one chapter is the labours in Mazandaran, another is Tahmina/Sohrab, another could be a later feat or early training.
Pros: Variety, player gets “tour” of legend. Could allow open exploration between episodes (semi-open hub).
Cons: Risky to mesh standalone segments; harder to find one coherent narrative climax. Nonlinearity might dilute emotional impact.

D. Framed by Sohrab:
Period: Start with Rostam in Mazandaran or birth, jump to Sohrab searching for father (player briefly controls Sohrab?), with story flashing back to Rostam’s feats. Unique but diverges from main hero role. (Also complicates player identity.)

Recommendation (Game I Scope): After weighing narrative impact and gameplay, we recommend Variant B – Rostam through the Sohrab Tragedy, with a caveat: integrate elements of his earlier heroism as playable content or flashbacks. In other words, Game I will tell the story of Rostam culminating in Sohrab’s death, but we don’t want to omit the Mazandaran labours entirely. Potential structure:

Act 1: Training and Call to Quest. Rostam, young and restless. A tutorial/prologue where he tames Rakhsh and handles a minor threat (or even the lion). King Kay Kavus summons Rostam (via Zal).
Act 2: Journey to Mazandaran (Seven Labours).
Rostam undertakes the perilous shortcut path to rescue Kavus, facing 4–5 key labours (lion, thirst, dragon, maybe witch and/or Olad’s rescue, and finally Arzhang or White Div).
Climax of Act 2: Defeat of the White Div, freeing Kavus (the Shah). Rostam is hailed as hero. (If budget short, we could reduce labours to 3 main fights plus cinematic leaps.)
Act 3: Aftermath & Family. Rostam returns in triumph. Scenes: The Iran rejoices, Kay Kavus is grateful. Rostam goes to Samangan (perhaps en route; e.g. Rakhsh wounded or war whirls?). There he meets Tahmina. (Possibly a side-quest: help solve Samangan’s monster problem before nightfall to win audience with the princess.) Rostam marries Tahmina (cutscene). Rostam must leave; Tahmina is pregnant.
Act 4: Sohrab’s Coming. Fast-forward ~15 years (maybe in title/time skip). Sohrab is grown in Turan. We might show a brief playable prologue of Sohrab (optional) when he is a boy/prince. Then Sohrab leads an incursion into Persia. He captures Iranian troops, leads to Rostam’s confrontation.
Rostam reluctantly sent to aid Iran. A confrontation grows.
Final Conflict: Rostam vs. Young Sohrab (first as armorless bullrush, then the real fight). After a tough duel, Rostam fatally injures Sohrab. Only after Sohrab’s death does the truth come (jewel on Sohrab’s arm).
Climax: Rostam realizes he has slain his own son. Tragic reveal.
Ending Variant: Rostam in despair (post-credits scene of Khalifing maybe), setting up sequel (player as Sohrab’s or Rostam’s perspective in future game, or Rostam’s final days). If we want a slightly hopeful note, perhaps scene with Tahmina mourning (from [24], she laments her loss) ending on a poignant line.
Trade-offs & Risks: This is an ambitious scope. It effectively contains two narrative climaxes: the demon-battle (White Div) and the father–son tragedy. We might need to trim or merge labours (4 labours in detail, others by montage). The Sohrab chapter would be the emotional finale. The risk is overstuffing the first game, but leaving out Sohrab would undercut why Rostam’s story is “epic tragedy” rather than a simple adventure. We’ll mitigate by early prototyping: for example, test if “Rostam rides after Sohrab” is feasible (since Sohrab was in Turan). Possibly keep Sohrab’s battle to a more linear boss-fight style (like a one-on-one duel through phases) to save development.

Decision Gates:

After developing Source & Narrative Bibles, decide how many labours to script fully.
Prototype Rostam–Sohrab combat early: is it satisfying to have Rostam kill his son?
Determine tone: are we comfortable ending on such a tragic note for Game I? Possibly run a story session with writers to gauge reaction.
5. Quest and Episode Framework
Main Questline (Game I): “Rescue the Shah, Then Face Your Son.”

Quest 1: Rostam’s Initiation. (Tutorial) Rostam must prove himself: taming Rakhsh and saving a village from bandits/wild wolf (first combat tutorial). Gains King’s notice.

Quest 2: Call to Mazandaran. Rostam arrives at the court of Kay Kavus. Briefing: Kavus is imprisoned by demons. (Cutscene: Kavus explains and punishes his advisor). Kavus sends Rostam on quest.

Quest 3: Seven Labours of Rostam. Multi-part chapter. Each labour is a main quest or boss fight:

Lion: While traveling, Rakhsh is attacked by a lion. (Tutorial combat #2). Rostam helps Rakhsh.
Desert Thirst: Rostam is stranded in a scorching desert. Gameplay: survival/stealth puzzle. He finds a she-goat leading him to water (per legend).
Dragon (Serpent): At night, a giant serpent attacks (Rakhsh is disabled). Boss fight: Rostam vs. serpent (using spear etc).
Witch: A sorceress (Div wizard) attempts to kill Rostam with spells. Mix of combat and puzzle: Rostam must destroy her to dispel magical roots. (In text, she is killed by Rakhsh; here the player kills her).
Rescue Olad: Encounter with Olad (brave hunter) who is captured by demons in a forest. Rescue mission side-quest that yields Olad’s alliance. In return, Olad (Allaudin) provides intel on demon fortress layout.
Arzhang the Div: Boss fight: a demon champion (Arzhang) on a hilltop, Rakhsh might be leashed, Rostam must fight him bare-handed (or use tree roots).
White Div (Div-e Sepid): Final boss of chapter: The White Demon King in his cavernous throne. Cinematic fight (with environmental hazards). When defeated, Kavus and survivors are freed. (Potential cut: create more manageable difficulty for solo dev team by splitting Arzhang+WhiteDiv into one climactic sequence.)
Quest 4: Journey Home / Marriage. Rostam escorts the recovered Kavus partway back. On the way (or immediately after), Rakhsh is wounded (justifying stop in Samangan). Rostam arrives at Samangan by nightfall. The local king welcomes him. (Sidequest): During the evening feast, Rostam learns of princess Tahmina’s request. The player may have an open-world option to explore Samangan, but keep it small (e.g. find cloak for Tahmina’s chamber, defeat a small monster at the gates, or choose dialogue options in feast). Cutscene: Tahmina’s seduction (as per Davis’s lines) and Rostam agrees to marriage. He must leave by morning (insert a quest: e.g. deal with a sabotage or diplomatic issue before dawn). Rostam is given a charm (jewel) for his child.

Interlude: Time Skip. (We may choose a quick montage: “Nine years later… Fifteen years later…” showing Sohrab growing in Turan, Horon (brothers) raising Iranian captives.)

Quest 5: Prelude to Tragedy. In Persia: Afrasiyab invades with young heroes (including Sohrab) to overthrow Kay Kavus. Or Sohrab rallies Turanian army to surprise Iran. Iran is on the ropes. Kay Kavus regrets sending Rostam away. (Possible side: Rostam hears rumors of a black-armored Turanian champion, seeks audience with Kavus who reluctantly allows Rostam to fight.)

Quest 6 (Final): Father and Son. Rostam travels to the battlefield (e.g. the White Fortress from Kavus’s Hamavaran war). Rakhsh by his side. Eventually Rostam and Sohrab face off in an open plain. Dialogue skirmish: Rostam formally challenges “young lion of Turan.” They duel (phase 1 without armor, phase 2 with armor). Sohrab disarms Rostam (per legend) but Rostam uses subterfuge and fatally wounds Sohrab. As Sohrab dies (cinematic), Tahmina appears too late. Rostam sees the charm on Sohrab’s arm. Climax: Rostam realizes the truth. Emotional cutscene as players watch father grief (Tahmina’s lament lines might be voiced). The screen fades on Rostam’s anguished face.

Optional Content:

Mini-bosses or mini-dungeons based on lesser stories (e.g. hunting down demons that escaped Mazandaran, retrieving artifacts from “Flying Fortress”).
Exploration: Hidden verses, shrines (Zoroastrian towers, tree of Simurgh legend) for world-building lore.
Dialogue choices: E.g. show mercy to minor foes, or question strategies (this doesn’t change major outcomes, but shapes how Rostam is perceived by companions).
Failure States/Player Death: Death can occur in combat (Rostam has multiple lives/ressurects by skill check triggers hero-blood motifs). Ideally we avoid permadeath; maintain checkpoint on village stages. Failure might restart a labour or final duel. Possibly an epilogue if Rostam lost Sohrab fight (e.g. Sohrab survives – but we should probably lock “canon Wins Rostam”).

Narrative vs Gameplay Balance: The story scenes (Tahmina, Sohrab reveal) will be heavy on cutscenes (featuring real voice acting) to maximize emotional impact. Most labours and the final duel will be gameplay. Aim: ~50–60% combat/questing vs 40-50% cinematic.

6. Rostam’s Psychology & Relationships
Rostam’s Character Conflict: He is caught between duty (to king and country) and personal life. He loves Tahmina but is duty-bound to leave her. He trusts his horse and weapon (and fate), but not men’s politics. His tragic flaw is unawareness and pride: he never considers that a bold young warrior in Turan could be his son. He treats Sohrab first as “enemy, ignorant kid,” even when Sohrab mentions “Your name!”; Rostam refuses to say “I’m Rostam.” Only pride and fear make him hide, and that seals Sohrab’s doom.

Key Relationships:

Tahmina–Rostam: Passionate but constrained by duty. They respect each other. In game, tender scenes (moonlit chamber) should contrast with battlefield.
Zal–Rostam: Warm, akin to teacher-student. Zal thinks strategically and is fearful of divine fate. Rostam, though loyal, sometimes brushes aside warnings (e.g. against Esfandiyar).
King Kavus–Rostam: Complex; Kavus both relies on and fears Rostam. Scenes: Kavus may cower or argue (“Go help Iran, you’re the only hope!” / “You are too mighty, I cannot risk you.”).
Rostam–Sohrab: Tragic unknown kinship. Early combat has mutual respect. After Sohrab’s final cry, Rostam’s disbelief turns to grief. Sohrab’s last words (“My father is Rostam”) will echo.
Rostam–Simurgh: Spiritual bond. Simurgh feathers (childhood and births) symbolize destiny. In game: if Simurgh appears in vision, Rostam is pious and humble.
Moral Core: Rostam’s story is about choice. His heroic actions (freeing Kavus) enable greater tragedy (letting Sohrab live). Player should feel the weight of choices: e.g. “Show mercy to subordinates or strike fear?” That may subtly affect later scenes (perhaps giving more time before Sohrab figure appears).

7. Dialogue & Writing Guide
Narrative Voice: Epic, elevated diction. Not modern slang or very archaic Shakespearean. Something like “By Ahura’s light, I will not let evil stand.” or “May the will of Ormazd guide us.” Short, impactful lines.

Dialect: Uniform high-register English. Some Persian terms allowed: “Shah,” “Div,” “Simurgh,” “Ifrit”. Honorifics: “Bacheh-am” (my son) in Persian (spoken by Tahmina or Zal), or “Khuda hafez” (go with God) to indicate cultural flavor.

Naming/Transliteration: Use consistent spellings: Rostam, Zal, Rakhsh, Simurgh, Sohrab, Tahmina, etc. The ‘Kh’, ‘gh’ transliterations (e.g. Seif/Kaus, Jahangir). Possibly include a glossary.

Exposition: Use discovery/environment cues. For example, short monologues by Rostam: “The mountains of Zabul… I remember them from childhood. Zal taught me the names of those peaks.” to gently provide background. Dialogue between minor NPCs or inscriptions in ruins can do the rest.

Sample Dialogue Excerpt (ORIGINAL):

Tahmina (soft, determined): “Champion, your name sings in our songs. I have waited on the border of dreams for you.”
Rostam (respectful): “Princess of Samangan, I am honored. Why does the tiger of Sistan sleep in your chamber?”
Tahmina: “Because I summoned him with words of longing. If fate grants me a child, let it bear your strength.” (She presents the charm.)
Rostam: “Then destiny itself has tied our threads. May Ahura bless this night.”

All dialogue should feel like translation of Persian poetry rather than modern talk, but clear. Characters do not recite exact quatrains from Ferdowsi in the game (avoid plagiarizing verses). Instead, convey similar sentiment. Use three- to four-sentence exchanges at most in key cutscenes.

8. Visual Storytelling & Art Direction
Overall Palette: Rich earth tones (gold, turquoise, crimson) for costumes; landscapes shift from the ochre sands and emerald oasis of Iran’s interior to the dark forests and luminescent caves of Mazandaran. Light is often harsh (desert sun) or mystical (moonlit forests, glowing demon lairs).

Cultural Specificity: Architecture like Samanid or Sogdian brickwork with blue tile inlays (as in Bukhara madrasahs) should inspire Persian cities. (e.g. Bukhara’s Abdulaziz Khan Madrasah shows brick arches and tilework that we adapt.) Clothing: flowing robes for nobles, leather lamellar for warriors. Jewelry and patterns follow actual Central Asian patterns (e.g. flower motifs, geometric borders).

Key Scenes (Examples):

Summit of Alborz at Dawn: Rukhsh’s silhouette with Rostam – establishing montage.
Desert Laboratory: stark dunes with a single tomb or deadwood (the lamb statue). No sign of life until water is found.
Div Fortress: A mountain castle carved from bone-like rock, torches flickering blue (conjuring underworld).
Samangan Castle: Lush gardens, reflective pools (water as life theme; Samangan was famed for summer retreats). Columns, decorated bricks.
Battle Plains: Open fields with banners, swirling dust. Iconography: lion emblems (Iran), eagle or dragon (Turan).

10. Risks & Open Questions
Narrative Risk: Balancing fidelity to Shahnameh vs creative needs. We’ve kept core events but invented connective tissue. If future research finds contradictions, we must adapt.
Cultural Sensitivity: We will involve Iranian/Tajik scholars to vet portrayals (e.g. Simurgh, Zoroastrian elements).
Scope Creep: Too many labours or quests risks an unfinishable project. We’ll set hard episode limits.
Emotional Tone: There is no gameplay payoff for Rostam at Sohrab’s death (he’s devastated). But this moment is central thematically. We must ensure players feel catharsis, not frustration.
Open Questions:

Should we implement an “honor/choice” system (e.g. rescuing Olad vs. leaving him)? (Game risk: extra design complexity.)
Can we portray the Simurgh effectively (VFX/AI)?
Localization: likely voice-over in multiple languages eventually (but initial script in English).

12. Executive Summary (Russian / Краткое резюме)
Проект «Рустам» – это стратегический документ для первой части серии игр по «Шахнаме» Фирдоуси. Игра – дорогое кинематографическое приключение с RPG-элементами, где игрок – легендарный богатырь Рустам. Сюжет адаптирует ключевые эпизоды эпоса: поход Рустама в Мазандеран (Семь подвигов) и трагедию со Сухрабом. При сохранении основных персонажей и событий, мы расширили сюжет игровыми вставками и персонажами.

Сюжетный охват: Рассмотрены два варианта:

только Семь подвигов (кульминация – победа над Белым Дивом и спасение шаха);
либо «полная» история с последующей битвой с Сухрабом (убийство сына).
Оптимальный выбор (рекомендуем): начать с подвигов (экшен-эпизода) и закончить трагическим эпизодом с Сухрабом. Это сочетает зрелищные бои и сильный эмоциональный финал.

Мир игры: Уникальная смесь легендарной дикой Персии и сказочных элементов. Мазандеран – мистическое царство демонов (точное местоположение неясно). События происходят в «догосударственном» античном Иране (куянинское время), в мире, где магия и зороастрийские мотивы – реальность. Акцент – аутентичная архитектура Центральной Азии, музыка с ритмами рубаба, нея и т.д.

Персонажи: Рустам – могучий, но человечный герой. Он ведет себя с достоинством и благородством. Сложные отношения:

с отцом Залом (мудрым, боится судьбы сына);
с женой Тахминой (страстная любовь);
с сыном Сухрабом (героическая трагедия невольного убийства).
Другие герои: царь Кайкавус (глупый правитель, разгневанный Демонами), Афрасияб (враг Турана), Симорг (волшебный великан-птица) и др. Персонажи озвучены по-английски с высокопарным стилем (без современного сленга). Примеры диалогов показаны выше.

Геймплей: Камера от третьего лица, упор на активный бой (комбо с мечом/топором, блок, специальные приемы Рустама) и атмосферные квесты. Основная сюжетная линия: захват Рустама на службе короля, поход через семь испытаний (каждое – отдельный эпизод/босс), возвращение домой и свадьба с Тахминой, а затем вторая арка с Сухрабом. Планируется вертикальный слайс (сердце боя) перед полномасштабной разработкой.

Итог: Игра должна установить узнаваемую мифологическую вселенную, показать красоту и трагедию таджикско-персидского эпоса на игровом языке. Мы избежим поверхностных «серо-азиатских клише», сделав упор на исследование и достоверность. Это не просто ремейк «Шахнаме» – это новый интерактивный миф, где игрок встанет на место древнего героя.
