2. Executive Summary (EN)
PROJECT RUSTAM will be a narrative-driven single-player action-RPG franchise based on Ferdowsi’s Shahnameh, centered on the legendary hero Rostam and his world. This document provides the factual and conceptual foundation for the game’s story. All key elements from the original epic are clearly labeled: [EPIC CANON] for events in Ferdowsi, [SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION] for plausible inferences, and [GAME INVENTION] for new material created for gameplay.

Major corrections since v0.1 include: removing the conflation of Sudabeh with Rostam’s narrative (Sudabeh belongs to Siavash’s story, not Rostam’s direct conflict), correctly identifying Rostam’s brothers Zavareh and Shaghad by genealogy, and verifying the exact sequence of the Seven Labours and the Rostam–Sohrab episode. We confirm, for example, that Rostam’s parents are Zal and Rudabeh, and that Sohrab is his son by Tahmina.

This Narrative Bible outlines the epic’s setting (legendary ancient Zabulistan/Kabul/Samangan, loosely inspired by Iron Age and medieval Central Asian culture), defines the main characters and their relationships, and assesses five candidate scopes for the first game. It recommends focusing on a single coherent story arc (candidates A–E are evaluated in a comparison matrix). The recommended first-game scope is [OPEN DECISION] pending approval, but likely will involve a heroic cycle culminating in a meaningful climax. Unresolved decisions include the protagonist’s precise journey (Rostam’s early labours vs. Rostam–Sohrab tragedy vs. alternate structure), the degree of supernatural elements in gameplay, and final production scale. These decision gates are listed below (see section 18).

3. Project Narrative Identity
Heroic Scale with Human Heart: The core narrative identity is “epic heroism grounded in personal cost.” Rostam is not portrayed as an invincible caricature; he is a “supreme hero of the Shahnameh”, but his legend comes at a great personal price. The game must balance cinematic spectacle (giant monsters, bloody combat) with emotional depth (family, honor, fate).

Cultural Legacy: The story is rooted in the shared Persian–Tajik cultural heritage. Rostam is “son of Zal and Rudabeh” – thus a Zabulistani (Sistanian) champion whose bloodline links him to the royal House of Sam. The game’s tone is epic and tragic, exploring themes of destiny, pride, loyalty and the bonds between fathers and sons (e.g. Rostam and Sohrab) against the backdrop of mythic Iran and Turan.

4. Canon and Adaptation Policy
Epic Canon [EPIC CANON]: We only present as canonical those events, characters, and relationships explicitly given in Ferdowsi’s Shahnameh. Example: Rostam’s birth by cesarean is canon; Sohrab’s campaign to overthrow King Kavus is canon.
Scholarly Interpretation [SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION]: When the epic is unclear, we rely on academic sources (e.g. Encyclopaedia Iranica, reputable commentaries) for plausible details. These are labeled “interpretation.” E.g. the exact geography of “Zabulistan” or the cultural context of Sistan is inferred from historical studies.
Game Invention [GAME INVENTION]: This label covers any new content created to make the story playable or to fill gaps. Examples include original side-quests, NPCs, filler dialogue, and any plot changes not supported by the Shahnameh text. These are documented in the Invention Register (section 16) and are not presented as part of the epic’s canon.
Examples: We will not attribute invented dialogue to Ferdowsi or add characters he never mentioned. If we introduce an event (e.g. a bandit ambush tutorial) that is not in Shahnameh, it’s GAME INVENTION. Conversely, if Ferdowsi explicitly names a character (“Olad” is a canon figure captured by Rostam), we treat him as [EPIC CANON]. Any speculative link (for instance, Rostam raising Kay Khosrow as a child) is at most [SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION] and flagged accordingly.

5. Source and Evidence Standards
All factual claims are traceable to authoritative sources per the hierarchy: (1) Ferdowsi’s Shahnameh (Persian text or reliable translation); (2) Academic publications (Encyclopædia Iranica, university presses, peer-reviewed journals); (3) Reputable educational websites (e.g. Shahnameh Atlas) for supplementary clarity. No Wikipedia, blog, or unsourced retelling is cited as fact. Quotations of Persian verse or names are taken from critical editions (Khaleghi-Motlagh) or Dick Davis’s English translation. For example, quotes from the Shahnameh used in this doc come via the Shahnameh Atlas (Moscow edition text).

6. Setting and World-Building
The mythic setting spans regions of ancient Iran and Turan. Key locations include Zabulistan (Sistān) – Rostam’s homeland – and neighboring realms like Kabul (Mehrāb’s kingdom) and Samangan (Tahmina’s land). The world feels ancient and exotic, drawing on early Persian/Iranian aesthetics: hill-country fortresses, caravanserai, mountainous passes, and mythic ruins. The exact historical period is indeterminate (blend of Iron Age and medieval influences), reflecting the Shahnameh’s pan-historical style.

We will distinguish mythic imagery from real historical culture. For example, we may use motifs of Achaemenid and Sassanid art for architecture (massive stone columns, reliefs of winged figures) without claiming Rostam lived in a specific dynasty. Musical themes will incorporate traditional Persian instruments (rubab, ney) to root the game in cultural authenticity. The presence of mythical creatures (Simurgh, Divs) is narrative license; their design must feel integrated (e.g. Simurgh as a giant firebird familiar from Persian lore). We will consult archaeology and art history for reference (e.g. Sistan-era sites) but allow stylization for gameplay and atmosphere.

7. Character Bible
Rostam – Protagonist. Canon: “son of Zal and Rudabeh”; supreme Iranian hero. Personality: honorable, proud but compassionate; struggles with war’s duties vs. family. Key events: Rostam tames his horse Rakhsh, performs the Seven Labours to save King Kavus, unwittingly slays his own son Sohrab, and later kills his enemies Esfandiyar and his half-brother Shaghad. Rostam’s tragic flaw: fierce pride and loyalty to Iran lead him into personal tragedy (Sohrab) and ultimately to betrayal by kin.

Zal (Dastan) – Rostam’s father. Canon: Legendary white-haired warrior, son of King Sam of Sistan. Raised by the Simorgh bird (canon). Martials; wise counselor. Zal’s role: sends Rostam to battle, supports the Kayanian kings. Loyal to tradition.

Rudabeh – Rostam’s mother. Canon: Princess of Kabul, daughter of Mehrab (a descendant of Zahhak). Defies family to marry Zal, gives birth to Rostam by cesarean (Simorgh helps). Gentle, strong-willed. Presents Rostam’s heritage: he is part Iranian (Zal’s line) and part “Zahhakid” through his mother.

Tahmina – Sohrab’s mother. Canon: Princess of Samangan; falls in love with Rostam. Strong and determined: she baits Rostam with praise until he agrees to an affair. She bears Sohrab and secretly retains Rostam’s token (bracelet). Motivated by love for Rostam and concern for her son. Tahmina is educated and wise. In the epic, her key actions: conceiving Sohrab, and later informing him of his lineage after the duel. In game: she may serve as a compassionate guide and the source of Sohrab’s conflict.

Sohrab – Protagonist or secondary hero. Canon: Son of Rostam and Tahmina, raised without knowing his father. A 16-year-old prodigy in Turan’s army, bold and tragic. He is noble and honorable; desires to make Rostam’s homeland (Iran) his father’s kingdom. Unknowingly opposes Rostam. In the story: he captures Hojir, defeats Gordafarid, battles Rostam. Sohrab’s arc is heartbreak: he learns of his parentage only moments before death. Motivation: ambition to claim a throne, love for his parents (conflicted by deceit by Afrasiab).

Kay Kāvus – Rostam’s king. Canon: King of Iran, father of Siyavash. Brave but arrogant and impulsive. He usurps the throne, makes rash decisions (attacking Mazandaran) and falls into a trap (blinded by White Div’s smoke). Rostam rescues him. He later causes Rostam/Sohrab’s tragedy by concealing Sohrab’s token (the potion). Gatekeeper of tragedies: his prideful mistakes precipitate the epic conflicts.

Kay Khosrow – Future king. Canon: Son of Kay Kāvus and Siavash; heir. Not present in the first gamescope except as nascent influence: Rostam respects his lineage and fights to protect his grandfather’s realm. (Kidnapped to Iran by Rostam after escaping Turan.) In the epic, Rostam mentors Kay Khosrow, but that is post-Sohrab. We will not detail Kay Khosrow’s story until sequels.

Afrāsiyāb – Turanian king, main antagonist. Canon: King of Turan, enemy of Iran. Grandfather of Sohrab (Sohrab’s ally/relative). Ambitious and treacherous. He orchestrates Sohrab’s invasion of Iran to dethrone Kay Kāvus. Key trait: he values power over paternal bonds – eventually executes his own kin (killing Nozar, Aghriras). Motivation: reclaiming Iranian throne, defeating the Kayanians.

Simorgh – Mythical bird-guide. Canon: Giant benevolent bird. Raises Zal in the mountains. Later returns to aid Rostam by giving magic feathers and the secret of the tamarisk arrow that can kill Esfandiyar. Spiritual symbol of fate/wisdom. Appears at key moments to steer heroes. We must portray Simorgh faithfully: as a divine creature of wisdom (not malevolent).

Shaghad – Rostam’s half-brother. Canon: Son of Sam (Zal’s brother) by a slave girl. Jealous and cunning. He conspires with the King of Kabul (Mehrab’s successor) to kill Rostam over a dispute about tribute. Final boss: he entraps Rostam in a spiked pit; Rostam kills him with his last arrow. Shaghad’s motive: greed and spite.

Zavāra (Zavareh) – Rostam’s brother. Canon: Son of Zal and Rudabeh, thus full brother to Rostam. Brave and honorable. He aids Rostam often. In the hunting expedition episode, Zavara is briefly captured by Rostam (a misunderstanding) but survives to serve Iran. Zavara is loyal; his daughter Zurangī is a warrior princess (side note). Zavara’s role: example of kinship; he ultimately avenges Rostam by capturing Kabul.

Other notable characters:

Gordafarid: female champion who fights Sohrab (canon). Brave and skilled. Could appear as a boss battle (Sohrab spares her life).
Hojir: Rostam’s ally; captured by Sohrab. He conceals Rostam’s identity (canon). Represents loyalty; later remorse.
Olad: a young Mazandaran warrior captured by Rostam during the Seven Labours. Becomes Rostam’s guide to the demon Arzhang. Friendly NPC.
Dragon, White Div, Witch, etc.: The various monsters of the Seven Labours (canonical opponents). Each has a distinct mythic lore from the epic (see section 9).
8. Verified Chronology
We confirm the broad chronology of Rostam’s life as follows (with canonical sources):

Birth and Youth: Zal marries Rudabeh and their son Rostam is born (by cesarean). Rostam is raised in Zabulistan (in a fortress or rural stronghold). At age 16 (canon, Shahnameh), Rostam tames the stallion Rakhsh (first canonical event). Shortly after, he rescues a young king (Kay Qobad) on Alborz and installs him on the throne – establishing the Kayanid dynasty.

Seven Labours: Under King Kay Kāvus (who foolishly marches on Mazandaran), Rostam undertakes seven trials to free Kavus. These are (1) Rakhsh killing the lion, (2) finding water in a desert with the help of a sheep, (3) defeating a dragon, (4) overcoming a witch/enchantress, (5) capturing the warrior Olad, (6) killing the demon Arzhang, (7) slaying the White Div, who was blinding the army. After the labours, Rostam defeats the Mazandaran king (who turns to stone) and installs Olad as vassal king.

Campaigns for Iran: Rostam next joins King Kay Kāvus or successor campaigns. He famously rescues the captured Kavus from the king of Hamavaran (Arabia/Hormuzan). He fights alongside or for Kavus and later Kay Khosrow against Turanian invasions (chasing Afrasiab’s hunts).

Meeting Tahmineh: After these wars, Rostam is dispatched as an envoy or pilgrim to Samangan. In Samangan, Princess Tahmineh asks for his hand and bears his son Sohrab (canon). Rostam leaves without knowing the child. He gives Tahmineh a token (bracelet or jewel) to pass to the boy.

Rostam–Sohrab Tragedy: Years later, Sohrab grows into a great warrior (raised by Tahmineh and Turanian allies). Manipulated by King Afrasiab, Sohrab invades Iran, hoping to find his father. In the ensuing war: Sohrab captures Hojir, defeats Gordafarid (a female hero). In single combat, Sohrab clashes with Rostam (neither knows the other’s identity). In the first duel, Sohrab gains the upper hand; fearing disgrace, Rostam feigns death. Rostam kills the demon Zhendeh-razm beforehand (canon). In the second duel, Rostam reveals himself and mortally wounds Sohrab with a thrust (canon). As Sohrab lies dying, Tahmineh reveals Rostam’s identity and gives him the token. Rostam is shattered. King Kavus arrives with a healing potion (nushdar) but learns the truth too late: he stops the cure, and Sohrab dies (canon).

Aftermath – Later Life: Rostam’s grief is compounded by other losses. He is credited with raiding Turan to avenge Iran (canon). He later fights King Kamus of Kashan and various demons (canon). Crucially, he is sent to retrieve Bahman (Esfandiyar) but ends up in a forced duel with the young Esfandiyar (grandson of Kay Khosrow). Following prophecy, Rostam stands down until Simorgh gives him a tamarisk arrow. He then kills Esfandiyar by shooting his eyes (Simorgh’s intervention) – a canonical event.

Final Betrayal and Death: In the last episode, Rostam’s half-brother Shaghad and Kabul’s king betray him over unpaid tribute. During a staged hunting trip, they spring a covered pit lined with poisoned spears. Rostam falls in it and is mortally wounded (canon). With his final strength, Rostam shoots Shaghad dead with a long arrow. Rostam’s death marks the end of an era.

This chronology is now locked to the Shahnameh text and parallels scholarly summaries. Any deviation (e.g. collapsing events into one timeline, inventing interim episodes) is labeled [GAME INVENTION].

9. Major Epic Events (with Canon Labels)
Seven Labours of Rostam [EPIC CANON]: Sequence as in Shahnameh. Each Labour is a distinct episode (monster fight). The final Labour (killing the White Div) liberates Kay Kavus. These trials can be played as a series of progressively harder boss battles.
Tahmineh’s Desire and Sohrab’s Birth [EPIC CANON]: Tahmineh voluntarily enters Rostam’s tent. This scene introduces the romance and sets up Sohrab’s birth. It should be portrayed as a brief but meaningful encounter, not trivialized.
Rostam–Sohrab Duel [EPIC CANON]: The three-day fight between father and son. This is the emotional climax. It includes Sohrab’s confusion, Rostam’s pity, and finally the revelation by Tahmineh. The duel’s secrecy is crucial: neither recognized the other beforehand (Rostam hides his name). Misrepresenting this (e.g. having them recognize each other early) would break canon.
Death of Sohrab and Kavus’s Betrayal [EPIC CANON]: Immediately after the duel, Sohrab learns Rostam is his father and shows him the token. Rostam begs Kay Kāvus to bring the healing potion from Zabulistan, but Kavus intentionally withholds it. This final betrayal is canonical and vital: it seals Sohrab’s death and Rostam’s guilt. In the game, the timing of Kavus’s arrival and decision must align with the text.
Battle with Esfandiyar [EPIC CANON]: The tragic fight between Rostam and the invulnerable prince Esfandiyar is canonical. It ends with Rostam mortally wounding Esfandiyar’s eyes on Simorgh’s advice. This could be a late-game encounter or cinematic (depends on scope), illustrating Rostam’s reluctant duty to kill a noble foe.
Final Trap and Death [EPIC CANON]: Rostam’s death by Shaghad’s trap is canonical. The betrayal by family and the dramatic final shot through the spears must be honored. This event should close either the game or a major episode. It is the ultimate tragedy in Rostam’s story.

Analysis: Candidates (A) and (B) offer the most coherent arcs. Scope C and E are overly broad or disjointed for a single launch title. Scope D is creative but risky (focal shift from the titular hero). If aiming for a single cohesive game, A and B are the strongest contenders:

Candidate A (Seven Labours) emphasizes epic action and gaming spectacle. It’s easier to build a scalable combat system and monster variety. However, it may lack an emotional “hook” beyond hero worship, and it delays the iconic father-son tragedy until a sequel. The Tahmineh/Sohrab subplot would come as a brief ending hook (Tahmineh’s letter at game-end).

Candidate B (Sohrab tragedy) prioritizes narrative impact, making the player feel the agony of Rostam’s mistake firsthand. This demands more careful pacing (we must justify Rostam being separated from Sohrab for years, perhaps via earlier flashback chapters or prologue). Gameplay could combine open battle with limited narrative sections. The risk is that many iconic labors and beasts are omitted, focusing instead on one climactic duel.

Given project ambition vs. feasibility, an integrated approach might work: start Rostam as a legendary warrior (maybe off-screen background of labours summarized) but let the player experience battles mainly in Sohrab’s invasion campaign, culminating in the duel. In other words, blend A and B: Rostam’s labours become prologue tutorials, then the main game is Rostam/Sohrab. This hybrid is [OPEN DECISION] for approval.

Recommendation (tentative): For Game I, focus on the Rostam–Sohrab narrative (B) while including selective flashbacks of key labours for context. This maximizes emotional resonance and franchise identity. The game would end with Sohrab’s death, leaving Rostam distraught (sequel hook). The Seven Labours could be explored later or added as bonus content.

11. Recommended Game I Direction (Provisional)
Working Title: Rostam – Rise of a Legend (tentative)
Premise: The player primarily embodies Rostam after he has become a famed warrior. The story begins with a brief prologue showing or narrating Rostam’s heroic past (e.g. meeting Tahmineh as introduction). The main act unfolds as Rostam defends Iran against the mysterious young champion Sohrab. The climax is the tragic father–son duel.

Narrative Tone: Epic, heroic, but inevitably tragic. The game should make the player cheer for Rostam’s victories, then feel genuine horror as he realizes he has slain his own son.

Game Structure: A mix of open combat scenarios and focused storytelling sequences. The structure might be:

Prologue (Tutorial): Rostam’s heroic past (e.g. fight a monster or two, meet Tahmineh).
Act 1: Threat on Iran’s borders – Rostam mobilizes.
Act 2: Battles in fortresses and camps (introduce Goudarz, Hojir, human enemies).
Act 3: The Rostam–Sohrab confrontation (the unmasking and tragic ending).
All major events in the Rostam–Sohrab story are EPIC CANON and must be included with fidelity. Additional material to support gameplay (e.g. village side-quests, Rostam–Tahmina dialogues, Olad as guide) are GAME INVENTION (section 16).

12. Character Relationships and Dramatic Conflicts
Rostam ↔ Tahmineh: One-night affair leads to profound bond. In dialog, Rostam is proud but kind; Tahmineh is respectful yet determined. Conflict arises from separation and Rostam’s innocence of Sohrab’s existence.
Rostam ↔ Sohrab: Core conflict (canonical tragedy). Father and son unknowingly at war. Dramatic tension: Rostam’s protective instincts vs. warrior code (he withholds identity until forced).
Sohrab ↔ Tahmineh: Son and mother share the pain of Rostam’s absence. Tahmineh worries about Sohrab’s fate. (She reveals truth only at the end.)
Sohrab ↔ Afrasiyab: Sohrab is a pawn in Afrasiyab’s scheme. Tension: Sohrab’s personal code vs. Afrasiyab’s ambition. (If depicted, Afrasiyab may be a distant authority figure.)
Rostam ↔ Kay Kavus: King and champion. Rostam’s loyalty is troubled by Kavus’s arrogance and moral failings (e.g. Kavus withholding nushdaru). In the game, Kavus may appear as impatient commander who urges Rostam into ever riskier battles.
Rostam ↔ Shaghad: Betrayers turned hunter. Final conflict of Rostam’s life. Shaghad’s treachery (setting the trap) is fatal and personal, but likely reserved for DLC/sequel (after main game).
13. Narrative Themes and Tone
Primary themes: Fate vs Free Will; Fatherhood and Legacy; Honor and Betrayal; The Cost of Duty. Rostam’s journey illustrates that even the greatest hero is bound by fate (his own dooms), and that honor (keeping one’s word) can destroy family ties. The tone is heroic but ultimately tragic. The visual and musical mood should shift from triumphant (vigorous Persian drums, bold orchestral themes) to somber (haunting motifs, lamentation) as the truth comes out. Emotional scenes (Tahmineh’s confession, Sohrab’s death) should be framed with respectful restraint, reflecting the dignity of the epic.

14. Dialogue and Writing Guidelines
Dialogue should reflect formal, grand speech (in translation). Avoid modern slang. Characters like Rostam speak in a straightforward, sometimes proverb-like style. For example, when offered a proposal, a Persian epic hero might say: “I have always earned glory with my own hands” (paraphrase of actual verse). Any invented dialogue should not contradict the characters’ canonical actions. Critical lines (e.g. Tahmineh revealing Rostam’s identity) must match the epic’s solemnity. The script will indicate source classification for each plot beat (see section 16).

15. Visual Storytelling Principles
Iconic Imagery: Rostam should have visual cues tying to Persian lore: his babr-e bayān (tiger-skin cloak), his great mace or scimitar, and Rakhsh’s distinctive bay-colored coat. Scenes should evoke Persian miniature aesthetics (archways, swirling dust, rich patterns) without copying generic medieval fantasy.
Landscape: Use Central Asian settings: jagged mountains (Alborz), desert wastes, oasis caravansaries, and ruined Zoroastrian temples. These will differentiate the world. For example, Mazandaran’s fortress might feature carved stone heads of devs, hinting at myth.
Monster Design: Demons (Divs) and mythical beasts should draw on Shahnameh descriptions where possible (e.g. the “White Div” is human-sized, painted white, wielding a mace). They must look otherworldly but not like generic fantasy dragons. We will consult Persian art and even Avestan descriptions to create unique silhouettes.

17. Narrative Risks and Open Questions
Faithfulness vs Gameplay: There is tension between epic fidelity and engaging mechanics. For instance, the Rostam–Sohrab duel is canonically fatal; adding a “multiple endings” choice risks undermining the theme. We have decided to avoid alternate outcomes for core events (See GI-05). Instead, player agency will lie in how Rostam fights and reacts emotionally, not in changing fate.
Cultural Sensitivity: Modern sensibilities might misinterpret the father-kills-son trope. We must handle it respectfully, highlighting fate and duty rather than gratuitous violence. The script should emphasize Rostam’s shock and grief immediately upon realization (as in the epic).
Production Scope: Even a single “Rostam vs. Sohrab” arc is large. We assume a small-to-mid sized development team. This means focusing on a few high-quality set-pieces (forest, fortress, camp) and not an open-world city. The emotional scenes require good voice acting and animation. We risk scope creep if we try to include all seven labours plus the duel; the solution is the prologue flashbacks model (only brief previews of labours).
