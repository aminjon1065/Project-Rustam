Executive Summary (English)
Research objective: A comprehensive study of the Rostam cycle in Ferdowsi’s Shahnameh, grounded in Tajik-Persian cultural context. We examine Shahnameh’s authorship, composition, textual history, and limits; reconstruct Rostam’s genealogy and the key episodes of his life; map the chronology and narrative coherence (and contradictions) of these legends; identify major characters, their relationships and motivations; and detail mythic elements (Simurgh, divs, etc.). Emphasis is on distinguishing primary epic material from later interpretations or expansions. We also explore adaptation opportunities: which episodes lend themselves to playable quests, boss battles, exploration, and emotional arcs (with cautions against arbitrary additions). Finally, we outline several plausible narrative scopes for Game I (e.g. Rostam’s rise and Seven Labors; including or stopping before Sohrab’s tragedy; later confrontations like Esfandiyar; or even a dual-perspective story), comparing them by dramatic completeness, character development, gameplay variety, faithfulness to sources, and production feasibility. Extensive scholarly and primary sources are cited. We note areas of uncertainty (dating, variant traditions, cultural interpretation) and propose next steps: deeper textual research, cultural consultation, narrative planning, and prototyping.

Academic secondary sources on Persian literary history, Shahnameh studies, and Central Asian culture (e.g. Fitzwilliam Museum exhibition essays, MELA review of new edition).

Comparative mythology sources (e.g. Arthurian parallels for the Seven Labors).
We prioritized primary epic material (Ferdowsi’s text) for plot facts, corroborated by multiple translations where possible. Historical and cultural context was drawn from philological and historical research (e.g. Central Asian archaeology, Sassanian chronicles, etc.). Any original game-design propositions are clearly identified as such. All claims are cited: e.g. Rostam’s lineage and key episodes from Shahnameh; text-transmission issues from critical studies. Uncertainties are noted.

2. Shahnameh Authorship and Textual Tradition
Ferdowsi and composition: Shahnameh (Book of Kings) is a monumental epic of ~50,000 couplets written by Hakim Abol-Qasem Ferdowsi (c.940–1020 CE). Ferdowsi, a noble from Tus (Khorasan), completed it ca. 1010 CE as a (largely) mnemonic retelling of Iran’s pre-Islamic legendary history. He drew on earlier sources, including the Sassanian Khwaday-namag, works by Daqiqi (10th c. poet), and oral tradition. The Shahnameh is often termed “the linguistic and cultural backbone of the Persian-speaking world”.

Structure and content: Ferdowsi’s poem spans mythical (creation to Jamshid), heroic (the Kayanid kings and heroes, e.g. Zahhak, Rustam, Sohrab), and historical (rise and fall of Sassanids) eras. It covers from the dawn of time to the Arab conquest (7th c. CE). Rostam’s adventures lie in the heroic-legendary portion, roughly the second half. The book’s internal structure is thematic, not chronological geography; episodes sometimes shift locales (Iran, Turan, mythical realms).

Textual transmission: No autograph survives. Early Shahnameh manuscripts date ~200+ years after Ferdowsi’s death. Medieval copyists “often smoothed over archaic grammar” or inserted interpolations. Modern editors rely on collating all available manuscripts (e.g. Khaleghi-Motlagh’s critical edition) to approximate the original text. We base our narrative reconstruction on such scholarship, aware that minor variants exist. For game design, we treat canonical episodes as fixed, but we note areas (e.g. chronology) where even the epic’s internal coherence is debated.

Editions and translations: The definitive modern critical edition is the 8-volume Shahnameh by Khaleghi-Motlagh et al. (Mazda Publishers 2022, reprinting the 1987–2007 work). The most accessible English is Dick Davis’s version (Penguin 2006, abridged for readability); we also used Helen Zimmern’s public-domain verse (1912) and archival translations. Where needed, we cross-check Persian text (e.g. Ganjoor database) to verify key terms.

Source limitations: The Shahnameh is a literary epic blending myth and history (e.g. it names historical dynasties but narrates fantastical events). It is our primary source for character and plot, but we recognize it is not a strict historical record. We distinguish:

Primary epic material: events explicitly in Ferdowsi’s text.
Historical/cultural context: facts about Sassanian-era Greater Iran and later Persianate culture, from scholarship.
Adaptation interpretation: narrative expansions or game-specific inventions we propose.
Uncited summary statements (e.g. “Rostam is Iran’s greatest hero”) are contextual or interpretive, with key details always cited (e.g. Rostam’s parentage or deeds).

3. The Rostam Cycle: Episodes and Themes
3.1 Genealogy and Birth of Rostam (Primary Epic Material)
Parentage: Rostam is the son of the warrior Zal and Princess Rudaba. Zal himself is the son of the hero Sam (Marzban of Sistan), a Kayanid noble. Rudaba is introduced as a princess of Kabul (Hamavaran), daughter of King Mehrab, who is said to be a descendant of the tyrant Zahhak. This lineage links Rostam to both the legendary Iranian past and to Turanian-Scythian heritage (through Zahhak). This “legendary Kabul” is a mythic place, not a modern nation; it indicates the shared Persian cultural sphere of western Afghanistan and Central Asia.
Union of Zal and Rudaba: Their romance is central. Initially, Zal (who had been raised by the mythical Simurgh) hesitates because Rudaba’s family is tied to Zahhak’s line. But the two swear fidelity. Rudaba’s mother Sindukht negotiates with Zal’s father Sam to avoid bloodshed; they secure peace and agreement for the marriage. This diplomacy motif emphasizes honor and alliance-building (rather than feud).
Birth of Rostam: Rudaba’s labor is extremely difficult (the baby is very large). Zal summons the Simurgh by burning a feather of the bird. The Simurgh returns to instruct them to perform a safe Caesarean to save mother and child. After Rudaba recovers, she names the boy Rostam (“Delivered, Released”), signifying relief from her pains. The Shahnameh explicitly links his name to this event (Rodabeh says “he shall be called Rostam… I am delivered of my pains.”). Rostam’s birth in Kabul also sends a silk portrait to his grandfather Sam, who rejoices at having an heir.
(Primary Epic Material) Rostam’s background illustrates how Ferdowsi blends myth with genealogy: the Simurgh’s intervention is supernatural, while Sindukht’s embassy grounds the narrative in regal protocol. We consider the Zal–Rudaba–Rostam birth sequence canonical and unaltered. This episode suggests themes of destiny (Rostam as a divinely-aided birth) and the hero’s cross-cultural heritage.

3.2 Rakhsh, Rostam’s Mighty Steed (Primary Epic Material)
Rostam’s legendary horse Rakhsh is introduced after his childhood. In the royal stables of Zabulistan (Sistan), a colt stands out among the herds. The keeper warns young Rostam that the horse is the “Rakhsh of Rostam” (known by rumor) and that only Rostam can ride it. Rostam mounts the colt; at first it rears, but upon hearing his voice it submits. This horse, with a “rose-red” coat, bears Rostam effortlessly. Zal rejoices and proclaims the horse’s name to be Rakhsh (“Lightning”). Henceforth Rakhsh is Rostam’s inseparable companion.

(Primary Epic) Rakhsh’s attributes: exceptional strength (strong enough for Rostam alone), intelligence, and loyalty. Only Rostam can ride him; all other horses break under the hero’s weight. Rakhsh later shares Rostam’s fate (he dies in the final pit with him). This bond is iconic: in any game adaptation, Rakhsh can be presented as a character (boss escapes, essential for travel, even mid-battle ally). The Shahnameh treats Rakhsh as more than a mount (divine favor protects him); but for our purposes, we note his introduction episode as a dramatic “taming the beast” motif and a milestone in Rostam’s coming-of-age.

3.3 Rostam’s Youth and Rise to Power (Primary Epic Material)
Early Feats: Rostam grows up in Zabul under Zal’s tutelage. By age 8 he is already mighty. The text recounts that after two more years (so ~age 10), Rostam single-handedly subdues the king’s enraged white elephant by club. He then earns his grandfather’s blessing as a warrior. Rostam’s youth is marked by extraordinary strength (he is “mighty of stature,” craving valorous deeds). The elephant episode establishes his character: fearless, self-reliant, protective of the realm.

Finding Kay Kavus: Shortly thereafter, Zal perceives that Iran lacks a king of the Kayanid line (the throne is empty after a turbulent succession). He dispatches Rostam to Mount Alborz to seek out the hidden heir, Kai Kavus (a descendant of Fereydun). Riding Rakhsh, Rostam journeys until he finds and enthrones Kay Kavus as Shah (after a brief meeting in a gardens). This cements Rostam’s patriotic duty and alliance with the young king. While this episode is depicted richly in the hero’s legend, for an action game it establishes Rostam’s status and loyalty to the royal house.

(Primary Epic) These formative adventures (elephant, quest for Kavus) set the stage: Rostam is now the recognized champion of Iran. They occur under the reign of Kai Kavus (the story’s chronology is legendary, but Kavus is son of Kawād, placing us in a ‘mythic Golden Age’). We note no contradictions in these early episodes: sources agree on sequence (elephant feat, then the Kavus mission). We cite Ferdowsi via translations: Rostam’s clubbing of the elephant and his arrival at the gardens of Alborz to find Kavus. Both are “primary epic material.”

3.4 The Seven Labors (Haft-Khān) of Rostam (Primary Epic Material)
Perhaps the most famous Rostam episode is the Seven Labors (Haft-khān), a quest to rescue Kai Kavus after he is captured by demons. According to tradition, Queen Sudabeh’s scheming and Afrasiab’s war lead Kay Kavus into the sorcerous realm of Mazandaran, where demons (divs) imprison him. The Iranian army is lost. Rostam volunteers to save the king and, accompanied only by Rakhsh, must pass seven deadly trials to reach Mazandaran. The Shahnameh describes each:

Lion (Sher): In the wilderness, Rakhsh is attacked by a lion. Rakhsh fights it off but is wounded. Rostam wakes, heals Rakhsh. Gameplay: This could be an opening challenge (protect the horse from beasts).
Thirst (Zū Isti): Rostam and Rakhsh cross a desert; after extreme thirst, divine providence leads Rostam to water. Not a combat scene, but a survival puzzle or hunting sighting (spotting the sheep).
Dragon (Ej Dev): A dragon repeatedly sneaks near Rakhsh. Rakhsh warns Rostam, who finally slays it. Boss fight: a serpentine dragon in a forest, showcasing Rakhsh as an early alert/combat partner.
Temptress (Morg Ab): Rostam finds an enchanted feast and a beautiful demon-lady. When he offers wine “in the name of God,” she reverts to a fiend and he kills her. This is like an illusory level with a femme fatale enemy. (This labor resembles Western Grail legends; notably Shahnameh scholars note an Arthurian link.)
Gorgs (Olad): Rostam conquers the champion Olad-Deev in Mazandaran, who then describes the demon caves. (Often not listed in Western summaries; in Shahnameh it’s an encounter with a demon-warrior.)
Rescue Kavus: He enters the city of demons, blinds Farud (mistakenly) and eventually slays the sorcerer king Arzhang, liberating Kavus (still magically blinded). This is climactic within Mazandaran.
White Demon (Div-e Sepid): Finally Rostam kills Div-e Sepid, the white demon chief of Mazandaran. The demon’s heart-blood restores Kavus’s sight. This is the boss battle finale.
(Primary Epic) The labors are vividly described in Shahnameh. We cite summary points to establish them as canon: e.g. Rostam’s conquering of Olad and killing Div-e Sepid. These challenges combine combat (lion, dragon, demons), environmental perils (desert thirst, illusions), and moral tests (Rostam’s faith and restraint). For a game, each labor can form a distinct level or episode, culminating in boss fights (dragon, temptress demon, White Demon). The narrative link is Rostam’s loyalty to Kay Kavus and his perseverance. The Shahnameh emphasizes Rostam’s heroism and Rakhsh’s loyalty through these trials. Notably, we must treat them as more than “checkpoints” – each labor has symbolic meaning (e.g. the temptress tests Rostam’s devotion to God, and the thirst episode tests faith), which could inform cinematic or dialogue scenes.

Continuity: The Seven Labors lead directly to the liberation of Iran’s king. In the epic’s chronology, these occur under Kavus’s rule, before the Sohrab saga. There is no contradiction: sources consistently place the labors as the mission to free Kavus. (Later retellings or folklore may expand or reorder elements, but for adaptation we stick to Ferdowsi’s sequence as “primary.”)

3.5 King of Hamavaran (Oman) and Sudabeh (Primary Epic Material)
After rescuing Kay Kavus from Mazandaran, an immediate new crisis arises: Kay Kavus marries Sudabeh, a beautiful princess of Hamavaran (identified with Arab tradition as Himyar, roughly southwestern Arabia). The Hamavaran king later treacherously captures Kavus (and imprisons Sudabeh) in collaboration with Afrasiab. Word reaches Rostam: “He cried and promised to bring Kay Kavus back from Hamavaran”. Rostam marches against Hamavaran/Egypt/Barbarstan’s allied armies, defeating them in pitched battle. He forces the king of Hamavaran to surrender: he will free Kavus and Sudabeh if Rostam allows the enemy kings to go (so they can continue fighting Afrasiab). Rostam accepts this bargain and brings Kavus and Sudabeh safely home.

(Primary Epic) This episode (often called “Sudabeh of Hamavaran”) is canonical: it follows immediately after the labors. We have it from Shahnameh summaries. It shows Rostam’s mercy and strategic mind (sparing former enemies to fight the real foe). For gameplay, it suggests a large-scale battle scenario (Rostam leading the army) culminating in capturing a fortress/city and negotiating surrender. Sudabeh’s presence adds emotional stakes (especially as queen of Iran). This also precedes the next generation’s saga (Kay Khosrow’s birth and the Sohrab tragedy).

No major contradictions here; it seamlessly follows Kavus’s rescue. However, note: Sudabeh of Hamavaran is different from Kay Kavus’s Persian wife (also named Sudabeh) associated with Siyavash – context must clarify.

3.6 Tahmineh and Sohrab (Primary Epic Material)
While Rostam is on campaign, his personal life unfolds in Samangan (northeastern Iran/Afghanistan). To find a daughter for the lost Rakhsh, he arrives at Samangan. There he sleeps; Rakhsh is stolen by local Turanian knights. The king of Samangan, aware Rostam is a guest, offers hospitality. In a famous scene, the king’s daughter Tahmineh (of Turanian stock) appears to Rostam at night, eloquently declaring her love and desire to bear his son. Rostam is moved by her beauty and wisdom. He obtains her hand with her father’s consent and recovers Rakhsh in exchange. That night, they are united. Before parting, Rostam gives Tahmineh an onyx armband for their future child: “if a son be born, fasten it upon his arm; he shall be strong as Keriman, graceful like Zal”.

(Primary Epic) This is the father–son tragedy setup. Years later, Tahmineh gives birth to a son, Sohrab. Tahmineh secretly trains Sohrab to be a warrior and tell of his father. Sohrab grows up unaware of Rostam, but eventually (as a youth) he leads Turanian forces into Iran to find Rostam and conquer. In their final encounter, Rostam and Sohrab duel, each not knowing the other’s identity; tragically Rostam mortally wounds Sohrab. Only as Sohrab dies does he reveal Rostam is his father. Rostam is devastated. (We will not retell the entire Shahnameh text, but this is the core story.) For adaptation, this is critical: it provides the emotional climax of Rostam’s personal arc. Sohrab’s youth (the “modern generation”) and Rostam’s guilt are rich narrative material. The source is unequivocal: see Zimmern’s translation for Tahmineh’s speech and Rostam’s gift. The epic calls it “a tale replete with tears.” It must be handled with care (cannot trivialize Sohrab’s character, as this is the thematic heart of the saga).

3.7 Rostam and Esfandiyar (Primary Epic Material)
Later, under King Goshtasp (Kay Kavus’s successor), Iran is at war with Turan. Esfandiyar (Goshtasp’s invulnerable son) is dispatched to subdue the independent region of Zabul (Rostam’s land). Despite initial negotiations, Rostam defies Goshtasp’s decree to submit, valuing honor and regional loyalty. The epic describes a prolonged duel between Rostam and Esfandiyar’s forces. Eventually Rostam seeks advice from the Simurgh. The Simurgh warns that Esfandiyar is fated to die if struck by an arrow, and that this act will bring disaster. Persuaded by destiny and fatherhood (Esfandiyar is Rostam’s friend’s son), Rostam ultimately shoots Esfandiyar in the eyes, killing him. Although victorious, Rostam is grief-stricken because Esfandiyar was a noble hero, and his death haunts Iran (Goshtasp had promised the throne to Esfandiyar).

(Primary Epic) This climactic conflict (“battle of Rostam and Esfandiyar”) is canonical. It occurs after the Sohrab episode (Goshtasp is Kay Kavus’s son, so in the next generation). It underscores Rostam’s tragic honor-bound character – he kills a fellow Iranian hero only because duty leaves no choice. For a game, this could be a major boss duel. However, it is extremely sensitive ethically: Rostam must be depicted as deeply regretful. (Historically, scholars note Esfandiyar’s quest embodies Iranian ideals; this fight represents complex moral themes.) We include it in “possible later game chapters,” not necessarily Part I, due to its gravity.

3.8 Later Life and Death of Rostam (Primary Epic Material)
In the final saga, Rostam’s half-brother Shaghad (son of Zal by another wife) conspires with the Shah of Kabul. Shaghad resents Rostam for not showing greater honor to his marriage (Shaghad had married the Shah’s daughter). The two lure Rostam (now elderly) on a fake hunting expedition. They have dug a deep pit lined with spears. Rostam and Rakhsh fall into it; Rostam is mortally wounded. Realizing Shaghad’s betrayal, Rostam shoots the arrow that kills Shaghad as his last act. The Shahnameh poignantly records, “This concludes the tale of Rostam and his horse Rakhsh”. Rostam’s body is later honored and buried in Kabulistan (present-day Afghanistan).

(Primary Epic) Rostam’s death is tragic and undisputed in the canon. It fulfills ominous prophecies in the epic (astrologers had foretold Shaghad’s role). It ties up all loose ends: Rakhsh dies with him (as promised by divine fate). The location of death (Kabulistan) underlines the broad Iranian cultural geography. For adaptation, his death would presumably occur at (or after) Game I, or serve as closure if Game I covers his full life. It is not to be treated lightly (a final cutscene or epilogue perhaps).

4. Chronology and Continuity

Notes on continuity: The epic’s timeline is thematic, not strictly linear. Rostam appears as the same man under multiple kings, and some kings (like Kay Khusraw) skip mention of prior events. We adopt a working chronology: Rostam grows up under Manuchehr, serves Kavus (his sworn king), and lives into the next generation (Goshtasp’s reign and beyond). There are no overt contradictions in the Shahnameh text, but some details (e.g. ages, reign lengths) are unspecified. We do note interpretive issues: for example, Rostam’s brother Zavara and adoptive son Siyavash blur family lines (Zavara is son of Sam; Siyavash was Rostam’s foster-son, not biological, with Iranian royal ties). We flag these as “scholarly uncertainties,” deferring to experts as needed. For game planning, this means events should flow logically, but minor chronological compression can be tolerated if it serves gameplay (e.g. aging Rostam through multiple phases).

5. Key Characters, Relationships, and Motivations
Below is a brief character matrix focusing on Rostam’s circle (primary epic characters):

Rostam (Rustam) – Protagonist/hero. Legendary Iranian champion of Sistan (Zabul). Son of Zal and Rudaba. Themes: exceptional strength, honor, tragic destiny. Motivations: protect Iran and his family; uphold chivalric codes; guilt over mistakes (e.g. Sohrab). He values duty over personal gain. Cultural note: Viewed as epitome of heroism in Persian literature.
Zāl – Rostam’s father. Son of Sam. Born with white hair, raised by the Simurgh. Proud and loving parent. He teaches Rostam. Often consults Simurgh (e.g. supports Rostam vs Esfandiyar).
Rudaba (Rodabeh) – Rostam’s mother. Princess of Kabul, from Zahhak’s lineage. She is cultured and brave (climbs tower by hair). Negotiates union with Zal’s family. Endures miraculous birth. Symbolizes compassion and agency (chooses Zal, gives birth with dignity).
Sam – Rostam’s grandfather, legendary king of Zabul. Blesses Rostam at birth. Loyal to Kayanid throne (assists Kavus). Dies in the wars. Represents elder generation.
Tahmineh (Tahmina) – Rostam’s wife (in Samangan). Daughter of Turanian king (i.e. Iranian-Turkic lineage). Bold and articulate: declares love and future son’s destiny. Bearer of Sohrab. Motivations: honor her promise to Rostam; raise Sohrab with pride.
Sohrab (Suhrab) – Son of Rostam and Tahmineh. Brave Turanian prince, warrior-poet. Unaware of identity. Motivated by filial devotion: seeks father and empire. Has tragic innocence (in fight with Rostam) – end of his arc devastates Rostam.
Kay Kāvus – King of Iran (Kāyānid dynasty) when Rostam is adult. Naive and ambitious (wants to conquer Mazandaran, is captured). Rostam’s liege lord. Marries Sudabeh of Hamavaran. After return, rules poorly (madness).
Sudabeh of Hamavaran – Princess, wife of Kavus. Initially passive, then forced into palace with Kavus. Rostam rescues both. Not to confuse with Persian Sudabeh (Kavus’s other wife in Siyavash’s story).
Esfandiyar – Son of King Goshtasp (Kavus’s son). An incorruptible, invulnerable Iranian prince. Rostam’s peer, becomes antagonist only due to royal orders. Embodies moral duty and tragedy (killed by Rostam).
Shaghad – Rostam’s younger half-brother (also son of Zal). Cunning and resentful. Plots Rostam’s death with Kabul’s king. Motivated by prophecy of his own importance and envy of Rostam.
Simurgh – A wise, phoenix-like bird that raised Zal. Appears at Rostam’s birth and his later troubles. A deus-ex-machina adviser (e.g. warns Rostam about killing Esfandiyar). Mythic element.
Divs and Demons – Generic adversaries (e.g. Div-e Sepid, Arzhang Div, Olad). Personifications of foreign/chaotic forces in Mazandaran. Mythic creatures.
Each character’s drama/conflict is rooted in loyalty, fate, and family ties. Rostam’s main conflicts: duty to king vs moral codes (Esfandiyar), fatherhood vs warrior code (Sohrab), familial loyalty vs ambition (Shaghad). In adaptation, we would highlight these arcs (e.g. Rostam’s horror on learning Sohrab’s identity).

6. Mythological and Cosmological Elements
(Cultural Context) The Shahnameh is steeped in Persian mythic imagery. Relevant elements for Rustam’s stories include:

Simurgh: A benevolent mythical bird (sometimes described like a giant phoenix) with healing wisdom. It nurtures Zal and intervenes at Rostam’s birth and in his duel with Esfandiyar. In art, it is often depicted as a guardian. In-game, it could appear as a guiding vision or flashback figure (rarely as a physical ally, to avoid distracting from human-centric gameplay).

Divs (Daevas): Demonic beings, often embodying chaos or paganism (as opposed to Zoroastrian order). Mazandaran’s rulers are “divs,” including the White Demon (Div-e Sepid) and the enchantress Arzhang. They use magic (e.g. blinding Kay Kavus). Combat with them is the focus of Rostam’s labors. In adaptation, their design can draw on Persian demonology (horned, monstrous, or corrupted beauties). We note these are literary symbols, not to be conflated with any real religion.

Dragons, Serpents, Enchanted Beings: Rostam’s trials include a dragon and sorceresses (the temptress). These reflect Indo-European hero motifs. The temptress (Arzhang) bears a lute and feast that vanish (akin to Western legends of magical banquets). We can depict such environments as “phantasmagoric” puzzle levels.

Heraldic Symbols: Rostam’s armor “Babr-e Bayan” (tiger-skin) and standard (likely colors) are iconic but not detailed in text. We should research Sassanian/Hephthalite armor for authenticity.

Cosmology: The epic assumes a cosmos with Mithra/Ormuzd and Ahriman, but Ferdowsi’s overt references to them are minimal. Rostam swears oaths invoking God, reflecting a syncretic culture. We will avoid over-explaining theology; these are background.

All mythic elements should be integrated through narrative context: e.g. treat the Simurgh as legendary myth, not a literal ally. Divs and supernatural are presented as fantasy foes. Emphasize that Shahnameh’s world is one where “magic” (beyond known physics) exists (flight, shapeshifting, divine punishment), so the game can safely include mystical levels, but must keep cultural designs authentic (based on researched Persian art). For instance, demons should wear stylized Sassanian armor, and Zoroastrian motifs could appear subtly.

7. Historical and Cultural Context
(Historical/Cultural Material) The Shahnameh is a product of the Persianate world, bridging pre-Islamic Iranian tradition and the medieval Islamic culture of Ferdowsi. Key points:

Geography: Rostam’s homeland is Zabulistan/Sistan (around modern-day eastern Iran and southern Afghanistan). Many places (Sistan, Turan, Mazandaran) are legendary. “Tajikistan” as a modern country did not exist; however, the entire region (Greater Iran) shared Persian language and heritage. We explicitly avoid nationalizing Rostam: he belongs to the shared Iranian-Persian heritage. Citations note that Ferdowsi’s epic “kept alive the knowledge of Iran’s ancient glory”.

Cultural influences: Ferdowsi drew on Sasanian-era sources (6th–7th c. AD). Characters like Kay Kavus and Goshtasp resemble Sassanid kings Kavad I and Hormizd IV. Our world design can reflect late antiquity Persian influence (Thessalian-style cavalry, Sassanian palaces, Hephthalite/Alchon motifs for Turanian horsemen, etc.). We will research Central Asian archaeology (especially Sistan valley and ancient Kabul region) for city architecture and clothing.

Tajik/Persian distinctions: The user’s Tajik heritage is central. We emphasize that Shahnameh is a common heritage of Persians and Tajiks, not “Iraqi/Persian vs Tajik.” For example, Samarkand and Bukhara (modern Uzbekistan) were also Persianate centers; Rudaki (mentioned in [4†L14-L23]) was a Tajik master poet. Linguistically, Ferdowsi’s New Persian closely matches modern Tajik (apart from script). So we stress “Persianate civilization (Central Asian/Iranian), where modern Tajiks belong,” so the game’s culture should feel familiar to Tajik traditions (music, motifs, lore) without inventing false “Tajik-only” elements.

Religious/cultural aspects: The epic is couched in a broadly Zoroastrian worldview (good vs evil, div-pazhoh), but Ferdowsi personally used mostly pre-Islamic Persian style. Characters swear by Ahura Mazda’s authority occasionally. We should carefully integrate any religious iconography (prefer fire altars, sun symbols) but the Shahnameh itself has almost none. Tajiks in 10th cent. were Muslim, but Ferdowsi writes an epic of pre-Islamic Iran. We won’t push either identity (no preaching); instead present a believable polytheistic folklore world with Persian names, dresses, and architecture.

Sources: The Fitzwilliam article and MELA review highlight the epic’s cultural role. Rostam’s Wikipedia (checked) notes his marzbanship of Sistan (Iran/Afghanistan). These inform our cultural framing. We carefully separate "Shahnameh’s legendary Iran/Turan" from any modern map. In world-building, we can label areas like Sistān, Māzandarān, Turān, Kabōl, with design cues from Iranian plateau and steppes.

8. Adaptation Opportunities and Gameplay Mapping
This section translates episodes into game design elements. For each key narrative segment, we note gameplay potential:

Rostam’s Origins: (Narrative/Flashback) Scenes at Zal’s castle (Zabul) can set up Rostam’s birth story (cinematic of Simurgh arriving, name-giving) and showcase young Rostam’s elephant feat (tutorial combat using club on elephant). These establish controls and Rostam’s strength.

Horse Taming (Rakhsh): A distinct scene where Rostam leaps onto the colt (maybe a quick-time event) and calms it (Steed-bonding mechanic?). From then on, Rakhsh is mount – possibly allowing mounted combat/travel.

Finding Kay Kavus: A quest-level: Rostam journeys through mountain passes to find the hidden king. Could include exploration (forest/snow level), minor fights (bandits or beasts on the way), and a diplomatic/parley cutscene introducing Kavus. Not heavy on combat, but story-critical.

Seven Labors: Each labor is a major set-piece:

Lion Fight (combat tutorial: protect Rakhsh).
Desert Thirst (survival segment: search for water or solve environmental puzzle).
Dragon (boss fight with a dragon; Rakhsh’s alerts lead to entering combat with special timing).
Temptress (stealth/puzzle: Rostam must not be charmed, maybe puzzle elements like playing lute, then fight a demon-sorceress in her grotto).
Olad and Demons (action: battle with Olad Deev, finding clues; transitional fight).
Rescue Kavus (infiltrate demon city, heavy combat with Arzhang Div boss).
White Demon (final boss: Div-e Sepid, huge monstrous enemy; cutscene of Kavus regaining sight).
These offer diverse gameplay: melee combat, boss mechanics, exploration of exotic Mazandaran environments (ruined cities, demon caves, enchanted forest). Cinematic dialogues (e.g. Kavus’s gullible schemes, Olad’s description) drive narrative.

Hamavaran Campaign: A large battle simulation level where Rostam leads Iranian forces on the seas (Hamavaran is possibly Oman/Egypt). Could be a raid/boss fight (ship boarding or cavalry charge). Afterward, a negotiation cutscene with the king of Hamavaran. If large battles are outside our scope, this could be condensed to Rostam fighting key champions (with large enemy armies as backdrops).

Tahmineh and Sohrab: The Samangan sequence: Rostam loses Rakhsh (tracks leads to king’s palace) – an escort or stealth mission through the city. Meeting Tahmineh: a rich narrative cutscene/dialogue (should honor her poetry and insight). Then fathering Sohrab (time-skip). For Sohrab’s story: we could optionally have flash-forwards of the boy training (as montage) or play as Sohrab in a tutorial (but not in Rostam’s game). The ultimate encounter with Sohrab is a pivotal boss fight with heavy emotional weight (Rostam fights an almost equal young warrior). Perhaps present choices (Rostam senses something, but the epic has no alternative outcome). After the fight, show the reveal and tragedy (animated or cinematic) – the Shahnameh’s moral turning point.

Esfandiyar Duel: If included, this is a high-stakes dual-boss fight. Rostam vs Esfandiyar can be staged as Rostam fighting Esfandiyar’s generals, culminating in the duel with Esfandiyar’s divine armor. This fight should be dramatic (perhaps at nightfall, on a riverbank as per the poem). Cinematic intermediate acknowledges the Simurgh’s warning. This could be saved for a sequel or DLC if scope is limited.

Final Betrayal: Rostam and Shaghad’s encounter – can be final boss encounter (pit trap). The game might end here as epilogue. The tomb or well can be a grim cinematic end. Historically, The Battle of Rostam and Shaghad is literary (the epic calls it “the end”). In narrative structure, Rostam’s death should only occur if the game spans his whole life. If Game I stops earlier, this is for future installment.

Exploration: Environments should be richly drawn: Zabulistan’s mountains, Samangan’s palace, the desert plains, Mazandaran’s demon lands (jungles, fortress), Hamavaran’s coasts, etc. Each location offers side quests: e.g. discover Persian temples, hidden Mazdean relics, cultural details (mini-historical documents).

Bosses: Key boss battles: Lion, Dragon, Black Demon, White Demon, Olad, Esfandiyar, Shaghad. Each has unique mechanics and symbolic meaning. Rostam’s foes tend to be either beasts or other hero-like figures. Boss design can incorporate Persian motifs (jewels, armor details). Cinematics after bosses (e.g. reading Kavus’s vision after his dream, or delivering Kavus’s throne) reinforce story.

Narrative Weaving: Dialogue and cutscenes should flesh out relationships: e.g. conversations between Rostam and Kavus, or Rostam’s silent remorse. We must preserve the tragic core (Rostam’s unintended patricide of Sohrab, his obedience to fate). Key dialogues in the text (like Tahmineh’s vow and Rostam’s gift) can be adapted nearly verbatim for authenticity.

Risks of Invention: We must avoid “fan-fic” additions that violate character. For example, inventing an ambiguous identity for Sohrab (player choice) would betray the fixed tragedy. Likewise, making the White Demon redeemable or giving Rostam a cheat to avoid Sohrab’s death contradicts the source’s moral. New content (like side quests) should not negate canonical outcomes. If we create new enemies or allies, they should fit the mythology (e.g. an original demon lord allied with Afrasiab). Any added subplot (e.g. Rostam’s early friend) must be clearly marked as “creative”.

We should emphasize progression systems: Rostam could gain new abilities or arms (his mace, bow, etc.), and even a developing skill tree (though the original story implies no real training – his power is innate). If we add RPG elements, they must feel organic (learning divine techniques from Simurgh visions? Possibly lore-driven upgrades). But gameplay fidelity is more important than strict realism.

9. Candidate Narrative Scopes for Game I
We deliberately do not fix a scope yet. Instead, we compare a few leading options for the first game’s story, all faithful to Shahnameh but varying in scale:

Option A: “Rostam’s Rise and Seven Labors” – Covers Rostam from birth through the Seven Labors (ends with Kavus’s return from Mazandaran). This is a coherent self-contained arc: hero’s origin → hero tests → triumph. Gameplay-wise, it includes training (elephant fight), horse taming, exploration, and the full set of iconic labors (boss fights). Dramatically, it sets up the character and Iranian world, but excludes family tragedy (Sohrab) and Rostam’s later conflicts. Pros: Focused adventure, strong early-game hook with escalating challenges. Cons: Cliffhanger without the famous Sohrab drama, and premature leave of unresolved personal stakes. Would require sequel for the big emotional payoff.

Option B: “Rostam through Sohrab” – Extends the story to include Tahmineh and the Sohrab episode (i.e. Rostam’s personal tragedy). This spans Rostam’s origins to the end of Sohrab’s story (his son’s death). This completes the classic “father-son” tragedy, giving full emotional catharsis to the main character. It includes all major early episodes plus the final duel. Pros: Dramatically complete (hero’s journey ends in personal loss); powerful emotional impact; Rukhsh’s arc and the sorrow give weight. Cons: Very long scope (may feel ambitious for one game); requires careful pacing (not all episodes suit one playthrough length). It also partially precludes Rostam’s death (would leave open his later life).

Option C: “Rostam’s Epic Life” – All of the above plus Rostam vs Esfandiyar and his death. This is the longest potential arc (birth to demise). It would require immense breadth. Pros: Definitive saga, “definitive ending” (hero’s full trajectory). Cons: Extremely large scope (maybe 3 full games’ worth of content); risk of “jack of all trades” feel; might overwhelm a single installation.

Option D: “Focus on Rostam vs Next Generation” – Alternate approach: center the game on the Sohrab sequence, possibly from Rostam’s perspective at Kavus’s court (with retrospects to earlier labors). This would be a smaller slice, ending with the duel. It sidelines other labors (presented as flashbacks). Pros: Intense father-son drama focus. Cons: Requires altering chronology, sacrificing some iconic gameplay (e.g. could skip a lot of labors action). Likely not preferred.

Option E: “Combat Anthology” – Frame the game as a series of quests/missions (like a mythic contract) not strictly in chronological order, each one a labor or battle, loosely connected by narrative framing (e.g. Rostam recounting tales to a scribe). Pros: Modular gameplay, focus on action. Cons: Low narrative continuity or character growth; would feel episodic and risk weakening tragedy.

Comparison criteria: We evaluate these by:

Dramatic completeness: Option B offers a full tragic arc. A ends abruptly. C covers everything but might dilute focus.
Character arc: B and C allow Rostam to evolve (youth→veteran→broken hero). A ends with Rostam at peak pride, C extends to his fall.
Gameplay variety: A gives varied quests (labors) and is manageable. B adds a narrative twist (Sohrab fight) but no new gameplay type (the final duel can be a boss level). C adds Esfandiyar battle (unique, but optional difficulty).
Source fidelity: All options adhere to epic events. C forces inclusion of every tragedy.
Cultural significance: The Sohrab episode is world-famous (Rostam/Sohrab). Option B is rich in cultural symbolism (fatherhood).
Production feasibility: A or B are safer (one to two hero campaigns). C is likely too large for a single release unless we plan multi-Episode seasons. Possibly B could be split into Part I (Rostam origin to Kavus) and Part II (Sohrab and beyond).
Given these, Option B (ending with Sohrab) is a strong candidate for “Chapter 1” of the franchise: it introduces Rostam fully, has epic battles and a personal tragedy. The ending sets up his future story (he now has a grudge to avenge, plus the burden of that mistake). Option A could also stand alone well, but leaves out the emotional core fans expect. Options C and beyond are better as sequels.

10. Unresolved Questions and Research Needs
Throughout this research, we note topics needing deeper study:

Manuscript Variants: There exist variant versions of some episodes in folk tradition. We must ensure adaptation uses the most respected rendition (Davis’ translation aligns with Khaleghi-Motlagh’s text). If multiple manuscript lines differ (e.g. small dialog changes), we default to the critical edition.
Dating & Chronology: Precise dating of mythical events is impossible. For narrative coherence, we may create a mythic timeline anchor (e.g. “Kavus’s 30-year reign”).
Geography Realization: We need detailed cartography of Rostam’s world (Sistan, Samangan, Mazandaran, Hamavaran). Historical geographers can help map these, but they are essentially legendary places. Our “world map” will be mythic (not pinned to exact real maps).
Cultural Details: For authenticity, we should consult experts on Sassanian-era weapons, clothing, and Central Asian motifs (e.g. petroglyphs, falconry, music instruments). For example, Rostam’s bow, his gauntlets, and his babr-e bayan armor – do archaeological parallels exist?
Music and Dialogue: The epic contains poetry (couplets) from characters (Tahmineh’s pledge, Sohrab’s lament). We should involve poets to adapt these lines, or use original Persian verse with subtitles.
Emotional Tone: The Shahnameh tone is solemn and noble. Any comedic or modern element (like trivial humor) should be minimal. Convey stoicism, honor, and tragedy.
Language: We may use Persian words for names/items (as Ferdowsi did), but subtitles or an in-game dictionary must clarify.
